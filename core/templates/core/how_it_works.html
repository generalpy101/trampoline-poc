How it works

The thinking behind every date the system shows. From the 30-second pitch down to the algorithm and the data model.

The opening

The brand promises every customer "10 to 14 days." That's wrong in two directions. Customers in a city with a warehouse next door could have had it in 4 days, and they leave for a competitor. Customers waiting on a delayed production batch actually wait 25 days and leave bad reviews.

This system computes a personalized date per product and pincode from data the brand already has: live inventory, scheduled production batches, and historical delivery times.

The algorithm at a glance

Given a product and a pincode, the system walks a decision tree of four branches and returns the first one that applies.

The four cases

BranchWhen it firesWhat you get
In stock nearby Stock at a warehouse in the customer's own region A single date, high confidence
In stock farther Stock available, but only at a warehouse outside the customer's region A single date, high confidence, slightly later
Awaiting batch Out of stock, but a production batch is scheduled with a known supplier A date range, medium or low confidence
Soft fallback Out of stock with no scheduled batch — or pincode unmappable A wide range (3–5 weeks) or a clear "not yet available"

The math

In-stock items

delivery_date = today
              + handling_buffer           (1 day, warehouse prep)
              + P80(transit_days_for_lane) (from historical deliveries)

Out-of-stock items waiting on a batch

earliest = batch_promised_date
         + handling_buffer
         + P80(transit_days_for_lane)

latest   = earliest + P80(supplier_slip_days)

Why the 80th percentile and not the average

Sort all past deliveries on a route by transit days. The average is the value at position 50%. The P80 is the value at position 80%.

If we promise the average, half our customers get disappointed by definition. If we promise the P80, about 20% miss the promise — and most of those miss by just a day or two.

The percentile is a single tunable knob. Raise it for fewer broken promises. Lower it for faster promised dates. We default to 80% because it balances both ends.

Why a range for out-of-stock items

In-stock orders have one source of uncertainty: the carrier. Stats on that converge quickly. Out-of-stock orders waiting on a batch have a second source: the supplier.

A reliable supplier slips by 1 or 2 days against their promised batch date. A flaky one slips by 7 or more. Collapsing that into a single false-confidence date wastes information the brand can use. The range width is the supplier's reliability made visible to the customer.

Pincode resolution

India has about 150,000 pincodes. Tracking delivery statistics per pincode would need years of data per zone. So pincodes are grouped into delivery clusters — about 2,000 zones with similar transit characteristics.

For pincodes we haven't seen before, the system tries prefix matching, walking from specific to general:

MatchExampleConfidence impact
Exact pincode110001 → Delhi NCRnone
First 3 digits (district)110099 → Delhi NCRnone
First 2 digits (state)11xxxxone notch down
First 1 digit (region)1xxxxx → northtwo notches down
No match at all880001"not yet available"

The point: a new pincode in a covered region still gets a real estimate. Refusing to commit just because we never saw that exact pincode would lose sales for no good reason. Conversely, a pincode in a region we don't serve at all gets a clear "not available" — not a fake date with low confidence.

Where the data comes from

SourceWhat it gives usRefresh cadence
Warehouse system Live stock per (product, warehouse) Real-time
Production system Pending batches: product, warehouse, promised date, supplier Real-time
Order management Completed deliveries: shipped_at, delivered_at, lane, carrier Daily aggregation into lane statistics

The first two are read live at request time. The third feeds a pre-aggregated LanePerformance table that's refreshed nightly. The estimator never queries raw delivery history at request time — it reads from the materialized percentiles.

The whole tier logic in 15 lines

def compute_estimate(product, pincode):
    today = timezone.localdate()
    handling = settings.HANDLING_BUFFER_DAYS

    cluster, match_quality = resolve_cluster(pincode)
    if cluster is None:
        return _not_serviceable(today, pincode)

    stocked = list(
        Inventory.objects.select_related("warehouse")
            .filter(product=product, quantity__gt=0)
    )

    if stocked:
        return _estimate_in_stock(today, handling, stocked, cluster, match_quality)

    return _estimate_out_of_stock(today, handling, product, cluster, match_quality)

Every branch returns the same response shape — date, confidence, reason, breakdown — so the front end doesn't need to know which tier answered.

Cache

Responses cache for 15 minutes keyed by (sku, cluster, match_quality, percentile). Inventory mutations invalidate every cached entry for that SKU. The match-quality bit in the key matters: exact-pincode matches and prefix-pincode matches produce different confidence levels and reason text, so they can't share a cache entry.

SQLite in production

A signal handler runs on every database connection and enables WAL mode, sets synchronous=NORMAL, and gives a 5-second busy timeout. The result: readers proceed in parallel with a single writer. This is the difference between SQLite being a toy in production and being usable for a small service.

How we know it's working

Every prediction is shadow-logged with its inputs, the tier that fired, and the date returned. When the order eventually delivers, the actual_delivered_at field gets populated, and a miss_days column is computed.

The Dashboard shows two things that matter:

  • On-time rate — should sit near the configured percentile (80%). If it drifts below 75%, the carriers or warehouses have a real operational issue.
  • Tier distribution — how often each branch fires. Sudden shifts here are leading indicators: a spike in "Awaiting batch" means warehouses are running short on a product category; a drop in "In stock nearby" means a nearby warehouse is struggling.

Where AI fits, and where it doesn't

Use AI for

  • Parsing messy supplier emails and PDFs about batch delays into structured arrival dates that feed the ProductionBatch table.
  • Generating the customer-facing reason copy so it reads naturally for any combination of inputs.
  • Drafting customer-support replies referencing the cached estimate.

Don't use AI for

  • The date prediction itself. It's a commitment shown at checkout — structured math is faster, cheaper, and more debuggable than a model that can hallucinate.
  • Cache invalidation. It's mechanical.
  • The tier routing. Deterministic if-else.

What would come next

The architecture deliberately stops short of a few things. The order they'd be added if traffic and data complexity grew:

  1. Precomputed hot-set. Identify the top product × cluster combinations from traffic data, keep them precomputed and refreshed proactively. Cold tail still computes on demand.
  2. Event-driven cache invalidation. Emit events when inventory crosses meaningful thresholds (in/out of stock, below safety stock) and recompute affected entries immediately instead of waiting for the TTL.
  3. Multi-carrier dimension. Statistics per (warehouse, cluster, carrier) instead of just (warehouse, cluster). Pick the right carrier per request.
  4. Learned model. Replace the per-bucket percentile lookup with a quantile regression model. Earns its keep once the bucket-based approach plateaus on accuracy.

None are worth building yet. The dashboard would tell us when they are.
